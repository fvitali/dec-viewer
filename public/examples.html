Dear ladies and sirs, here you can find a few examples of RDF sets exploring DEC modal reasoning for subjective states of minds and representation of disgreements, uncertainties and lies.

All these examples are available using named graphs, rdf star triples and reification triples.

Here you will find a few examples using classes with arbitrary names and exactly ONE individual (entityX) to explore in the simplest way the different types of reasoning affored by DEC universes.

There are several DEC graphs each exploring a different type of DEC reasoning. Graph g1 is shared knowledge and provides content for all graphs (except verbatim). Graph g2 is verbatim, and does not interact with the other graphs and performs no inferences. Graph g3 is doxastic, does not interact with the other graphs but uses the shared knowledge and performs local inferences. Graph g4 is epistemic, and generates true facts. It uses the shared knowledge, and all its inferences permeate into the default graph. Graph g5 is conjectural, and adds additional statements to true facts. It uses the shared knowledge and the default graph, but does not permeate to anywhere. The default graph permeate from the shared knowledge and epistemic graph g4, and permeates into conjectural graph g5.

GRAPH ex:g1 { ex:entityX rdf:type ex:A . ex:entityY rdf:type ex:Z. } ex:g1 rdf:type dec:sharedUniverse . ex:A rdfs:subClassOf ex:B . GRAPH ex:g2 { ex:A rdfs:subClassOf ex:C . } ex:g2 rdf:type dec:verbatimUniverse . GRAPH ex:g3 { ex:A rdfs:subClassOf ex:D . } ex:g3 rdf:type dec:doxasticUniverse . GRAPH ex:g4 { ex:A rdfs:subClassOf ex:E . } ex:g4 rdf:type dec:epistemicUniverse . GRAPH ex:g5 { ex:A rdfs:subClassOf ex:F . } ex:g5 rdf:type dec:conjecturalUniverse .

Graph g1 (shared) does not change. Graph g2 (verbatim) does not change. Graph g3 (doxastic) permeates from the shared knowledge g1, and therefore asserts that entityX is of type A (from shared) and of type D (from itself), but not of type B (from default graph). The default graph permeates from the shared knowledge g1, and from the epistemic graph g4, therefore asserts that entityX is of type A (from shared), of type B (from itself), and of type E (from g4). Graph g5 (conjectural) permeates from the shared knowledge g1 and from the default graph, and therefore asserts that entityX is of type A (from shared), of type B (from the default graph), and of type E (from itself). The default graph permeates from the shared knowledge g1, and from the epistemic graph g4, but not from the conjectural graph g5, therefore asserts that entityX is of type A (from shared), of type B (from itself) and of type E (from epistemic graph g4).

Some basic facts and opinions about the world of animals. Some statements are known to all, some are not.

Alice says that dolphins are mammals.

:Flipper a zoo:Dolphin. zoo:Mammal rdfs:subClassOf zoo:Animal. GRAPH :g1 { zoo:Dolphin rdfs:subClassOf zoo:Mammal. } :g1 a dec:verbatimUniverse. :alice :says :g1.

Alice says that dolphins are mammals. Yet, although mammals are animals, Alice does not say that dolphins are mammals. No inference on Alice's words is performed, because she did not say the consequences of her words.

Bruce is not very expert in zoology, and believes that whales are fishes.

GRAPH :g2 { zoo:Whale rdfs:subClassOf zoo:Fish. } :bruce :believes :g2. :believes a dec:doxasticPredicate . GRAPH :shared { :mobydick a zoo:Whale. zoo:Fish rdfs:subClassOf zoo:Animal. zoo:Mammal rdfs:subClassOf zoo:Animal. } :shared a dec:sharedUniverse .

We all know that Moby Dick is a whale. Yet, Bruce believes that whales are fishes (how foolish!), and therefore, Moby Dick is a fish.

Catherine is correct in her knowledge. Moby Dick is indeed a mammal.

GRAPH :g2 { zoo:Whale rdfs:subClassOf zoo:Mammal. } :catherine :knows :g2. :knows a dec:epistemicPredicate . GRAPH :shared { :mobydick a zoo:Whale. zoo:Fish rdfs:subClassOf zoo:Animal. zoo:Mammal rdfs:subClassOf zoo:Animal. } :shared a dec:sharedUniverse .

Moby Dick is a whale. This is a fact known to everybody. Catherine is aware that whales are mammals. Since it is specified inside an epistemic universe, this also ends up being a fact. Therefore, Moby Dick is a whale, a mammal and an animal.

Superman's paradox is a well known paradox in logic. Lois Lane, Superman's fiancee, knows that Superman can fly. Since Superman and Clark Kent are the same person, does she know that Clark Kent can fly? We show the same example in three syntaxes: rdf-star, reification and named graphs.

This is the usual representation of the paradox of Superman using rdf-star triples.

:superman owl:sameAs :clarkkent. :loislane :thinks << :superman :can :fly >>.

One may be tempted to deduce that Lois Lane thinks that Clark Kent can fly. Yet, by default we treat rdf-star statements as verbatim, there is no inference and no paradox here.

This is the usual representation of the paradox of Superman using a reification syntax.

:superman owl:sameAs :clarkkent. :loislane :thinks :S1. :S1 rdf:type rdf:Statement. :S1 rdf:Subject :superman. :S1 rdf:Predicate :can. :S1 rdf:Object :fly.

One may be tempted to deduce that Lois Lane thinks that Clark Kent can fly. Yet, by default we treat rdf-star statements as verbatim, there is no inference and no paradox here.

This is the usual representation of the paradox of Superman using a named graph.

:superman owl:sameAs :clarkkent. :loislane :thinks :S1 GRAPH :S1 { :superman :can :fly. }

One may be tempted to deduce that Lois Lane thinks that Clark Kent can fly. Yet, by default we treat rdf-star statements as verbatim, there is no inference and no paradox here.

This is a doxastic version of Superman's problem. A doxastic predicate seems the best choice in this case, because it introduces a subjective universe completely separate from reality and independent from it.

:superman owl:sameAs :clarkkent. :loislane :thinks << :superman :can :fly >>. :thinks a dec:doxasticPredicate. GRAPH :shared { :Superhero owl:equivalentClass [ a owl:Restriction ; owl:onProperty :can ; owl:hasValue :fly ] . } :shared a dec:sharedUniverse.

Predicate :think is specified as a doxastic predicate, so it does not allow permeation to and from reality even if inferences are allowed. yet, no paradox takes place.

The only way to generate a Superman's paradox with DEC is to assume that Lois Lane's thoughts actually permeate facts from reality (i.e., they are conjectures). This choice would indeed generate the paradox.

:superman owl:sameAs :clarkkent. :loislane :thinks << :superman :can :fly >>. :thinks a dec:conjecturalPredicate.

Predicate :think is now specified as an conjectural predicate, which permeates to reality. Therefore, we are required to conclude that Lois Lane thinks that Clark Kent can fly.

This is an exactly equivalent dataset that uses subclasses instead of sameAs relationships, and yet generate an equivalent paradox. It is not a referential problem, but an inferential one!

:SuperHero rdfs:subClassOf :FictionalBeing. :loislane :thinks << :superman a :SuperHero >>

One may be tempted to deduce that Lois Lane thinks that Superman is a fictional being. This also is a paradox, but it is not a referential one: blocking a name change does not affect the paradoxicality of the statement.

Again, if we choose a doxastic relationship, the paradox disappears.

:thinks a dec:doxasticPredicate. :SuperHero rdfs:subClassOf :FictionalBeing. :loislane :thinks << :superman a :SuperHero >>

Again, the only way to generate the paradox is using a conjecture.

:thinks a dec:conjecturalPredicate. :SuperHero rdfs:subClassOf :FictionalBeing. :loislane :thinks << :superman a :SuperHero >>

Shakespeare authorship question is the argument that someone other than William Shakespeare of Stratford-upon-Avon wrote the works attributed to him. Delia Bacon's The Philosophy of the Plays of Shakespeare Unfolded (1857) proposed the first "group theory". It attributed the works of Shakespeare to "a little clique of disappointed and defeated politicians" led by Sir Walter Raleigh which included Sir Francis Bacon and perhaps Edmund Spenser, Lord Buckhurst, and Edward de Vere. Gilbert Slater's The Seven Shakespeares (1931) proposed that the works were written by seven different authors: Francis Bacon, Edward de Vere, Sir Walter Raleigh, William Stanley, Christopher Marlowe, Mary Sidney, and Roger Manners In the early 1960s, Edward de Vere, Francis Bacon, Roger Manners, William Herbert and Mary Sidney were suggested as members of a group referred to as "The Oxford Syndicate". Christopher Marlowe, Robert Greene and Thomas Nashe have also been proposed as participants. Francis Bacon was proposed as sole author by William Henry Smith in 1856. A case for Edward De Vere, 17th Earl of Oxford's, authorship was discusssed by J. Thomas Looney (1920). Christopher Marlowe was first proposed as the sole author of Shakespeare's "stronger plays" in 1895 by Wilbur G. Zeigler. William Stanley, 6th Earl of Derby, was first proposed as a candidate in 1891 by James Greenstreet. Although the idea has attracted much public interest, all but a few Shakespeare scholars and literary historians consider it a fringe theory, and for the most part acknowledge it only to rebut or disparage the claims.

Flavius Eutropius, a historian who lived in Rome and Constantinople between 363 and 387 AD, wrote about the building of a wall in the northern part of Britain, under the orders of Septimius Severus, the Roman emperor who reigned between 193 and 211 AD. The venerable Bede, the English monk and historian who lived between 672 and 735 AD, tried to make sense of Eutropius' claim of a wall built by Severus, and since he knew the one much further north had been built by Antoninus Pius (142 AD), he inferred that the wall by Severus mentioned by Eutropius was the one much further south. In 1840 priest and historian John Hodgson, while writing his magnus opus "History of Northumberland," challenged the long-standing attribution of the wall to Severus. Since the book he was writing only tangentially regarded the wall, he devoted a single footnote in volume III to his doubts. But since he was a thorough scholar, this footnote is a remarkable 173 pages long and provides ground-breaking evidence supporting the attribution of the wall to Hadrian rather than Severus. Hodgson's ideas became accepted and adopted as the correct attribution of the wall, up to this day.

The traditional attribution of the painting "Salvator Mundi" was to Bernardino Luini. In 1958 it was sold for £45 as a work by Boltraffio. After heavy restoration works by Dianne Dwyer Modestini in 2006-2011, the attribution to Leonardo da Vinci was proposed and discussed. Under this attribution, the painting was sold in 2017 for 450 US$ millions. The attribution to Leonardo divided the scholars in three main groups. Full attribution to Leonardo: Dianne Dwyer Modestini, Nicholas Penny, Martin Kemp, Walter Isaacson, Nicholas Hall and many others, all believe the painting "Salvator Mundi" is by Leonardo da Vinci. Partial attribution to Leonardo: Carmen Bambach attributes much to Boltraffio and only some retouchings to Leonardo. Matthew Landrus thinks it is a work of Bernardino Luini with 5% by Leonardo. Frank Zöllner thinks it was mostly done by a pupil of Leonardo, with distinct retouchings by the restorer Dianne Dwyer Modestini. Rejection of attribution: Jacques Franck believes the painting to be jointly by Salai and Boltraffio. Michael Daley thinks it is the model of a painting used by Leonardo for his own.
